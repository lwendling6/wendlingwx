What this is

You text a short request from your inReach. A free service pulls a high resolution weather model for your exact spot, packs a full forecast into a few messages of coded letters, and sends them back. This app builds the request and reads the reply. It works entirely offline once installed.

Which model to pick

48 h HRRR-AK. The highest resolution model available for Alaska, on a 3 km grid, so it actually resolves terrain: inlet channeling, gap winds, the difference between a valley and the ridge above it. Best choice for anything inside two days. This is the only option that includes winds aloft.

5 day. HRRR-AK for the first 48 hours, then a global model blend for days 3 to 5. The far days are coarse, roughly 11 to 13 km, which is fine for "is a system coming" and poor for timing. Use it for planning, not commitment.

5 day NWS. The National Weather Service forecast grids, which are model output adjusted by Alaska forecasters who know the local biases. Different failure modes than raw model output. When it and HRRR-AK agree, trust goes up, and that is exactly why both exist here. No winds aloft.

Which reply length to pick

Every reply carries the same fields. Length buys time resolution, not more information per step. On an inReach each message counts against your plan, so Basic is the cheap habit.

Winds aloft are only available from HRRR-AK, and only within 48 hours. They appear on every HRRR-AK segment regardless of reply length, at 3,000, 6,000 and 10,000 ft above sea level. The NWS option and days 3 to 5 of the 5 day option do not carry them, because those sources do not provide them.

Before every trip, in town

  1. Open this app in airplane mode. If it does not load, open it once with signal and try again. Do not leave until it loads offline.
  2. Save the wx address as a contact on the inReach, or in the Garmin app, whichever you send from.
  3. Send one test request from the trailhead and decode the reply, so you know the whole loop is alive before you need it.

Requesting a forecast

  1. Pick the model and the reply length.
  2. Tap Use my location. Step into the open if it fails. GPS works in airplane mode.
  3. Tap Copy message and send it to your wx address.
  4. Leave location sharing on if you can. The service then uses the inReach's own coordinates, which are more precise than a phone fix under trees. If you keep location off, the coordinates in the message text are used instead and everything still works.
  5. Wait. Ten to twenty minutes is normal.

Reading the reply

  1. Copy each reply message.
  2. Open Decode and paste each one into its own box. Order does not matter. Add boxes if you asked for more than two.
  3. Tap Read forecast.

If it says a part is missing, one message has not arrived yet. If it says the checksum failed, a message got garbled or truncated: re-copy each one in full and try again.

Reading the display

Each row is one time step, with the temperature and a weather icon on the left. Wind is spelled out, like "from the west-southwest at 18 mph, gusts 31." Precipitation shows amount and type. The banner near the top compares the freezing level to your ground elevation, and turns red when it reaches you. Winds aloft, when present, sit on the small line beneath each row.

If things go wrong

  1. No reply after 30 minutes: resend. Satellite messages do go missing. A reply saying the forecast failed means try again in 15 minutes.
  2. Reply is readable text, not code: the service hit an error and is telling you what happened.
  3. Phone dead: the coded reply is unreadable without this app. Treat this as a supplement, never the plan.
This is a hobby service with no guarantee. It depends on free tiers of several services and a weather model that is wrong sometimes, especially in valleys during inversions. Never bet a river crossing, a flight, or an exposed ridge on a single forecast from anything, including this.

Forecast data comes from public sources, free of charge:

  • NOAA / NCEP HRRR-AK, the 3 km High-Resolution Rapid Refresh Alaska model, for the first 48 hours, retrieved from the NOAA Open Data Dissemination archive on AWS.
  • Open-Meteo, blending global models including NOAA GFS and DWD ICON, for days 3 through 5.
  • NOAA National Weather Service forecast grids for the NWS option.
  • Copernicus GLO-90 elevation data, via Open-Meteo, used to correct temperatures to your actual ground height.

Model data is downloaded and packed by Herbie and pygrib. None of these organisations endorse or have any involvement with this tool, and any errors in the forecast you see here are this tool's, not theirs.
